Quantitative Cell Morphology Extraction using Image Segmentation

Bennet Plucienik-Kapoun

Problem Statement

Microscopy images are widely used in medicine, biology, and healthcare to study cell behavior, response to medicine or drugs, and overall health. A fundamental part of these studies is the ability to individually identify cells and extract their morphology from larger biological images.

However, these images often contain many cells, including cells that overlap, touch, or appear under poor lighting conditions. Noise and inconsistent staining can also make segmentation difficult. These factors make it harder than it may initially seem to automatically gather useful information from microscopy images.

The goal of this project is to create a computer vision program that segments individual cells from biological images and extracts morphological measurements such as roundness, size, boundaries, and shape. Ultimately, this project aims to reduce the need for manual identification and make the process more automatic.

Motivation

Cell morphology is strongly related to biological state. For example, a cell may be dividing, shrinking during apoptosis, or showing irregular boundaries due to disease. Even simple measurements such as cell shape and size can provide important information about a sample.

I am interested in this project because it combines my computer science background with my interest in healthcare and biomedical engineering. Many people in my family work in healthcare, and I am interested in developing technologies that could help support early disease detection and prevention.

Current State-of-the-Art

Modern cell segmentation methods are often dominated by deep learning approaches. These methods can be highly accurate, but they commonly require large training datasets, parameter tuning, and GPU resources. This can make them difficult to use in smaller settings where data and computing resources are limited.

Classical image processing methods such as thresholding, watershed segmentation, edge detection, and morphological operations are still useful because they are fast, understandable, and do not require the same amount of training data as deep neural networks.

Approach

This project begins with a baseline classical image processing pipeline. The image is denoised and normalized, followed by thresholding and morphological cleanup. Watershed segmentation is then used to separate touching cells, and the final labeled regions are used to extract morphology features.

The proposed approach focuses not only on brightness, but also on object shape and boundary information. This allows the method to better handle cells that are touching or overlapping compared to simpler threshold-only approaches.
